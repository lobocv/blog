# Voice

How Calvin wants his words to come across anywhere on the site: posts, project
pages, home and journey copy, bios and social posts. Use it when critiquing his
drafts and when writing anything in his voice (eg project pages, UI copy).

The mechanical rules (em-dashes, banned words, AI tells) live in
`docs/writing.md`, "Sounding human, not AI". This file is about tone.

## Goals

- Casual but informative
- Humble, not boastful or show-offy
- Not preachy
- Curious and optimistic

## What already works

Keep doing these. They are the voice at its best:

- Owning the experience instead of claiming expertise: "I've felt this
  exhaustion myself", "After working with the language for several years, I have
  come to love the way Go approaches error handling"
- Admitting where he lost: "I've thought a lot about how to write documentation
  in a way that ages well, but like all battles with time, you end up losing"
- Being fair to the other side before making a point: "To be fair, humans don't
  always speak the same language", "Regardless of how you feel about it"
- Wry asides in brackets: "(in reality, not nearly enough)", "(in both the
  literal and metaphorical sense)"
- Asking the reader's question for them: "But what happens if there is a
  subscriber that wants to react to a group of events as an aggregate?"
- Plain, friendly openers: "Being a software developer can be overwhelming at
  times"

## Where drafts drift

### Away from casual

- Long, noun-heavy sentences that read like a spec (eg "This alternative index
  into the system is a translation layer from domain language to code that puts
  no constraints on how that code is structured, organized or represented on the
  file system"). Split them and lead with the plain version
- Long lists of nouns (eg "the architecture, constraints, terminology, user
  experience and product vision"). Pick the one or two that matter for the point.
  A list of five reads like keywords
- Resume language: "systems that scale", "scaling teams and products", and
  definitions like "Technical leadership means...". Say what he does, plainly

### Away from humble

- Promises that sound like a pitch (eg "an approach that doesn't compromise
  speed for quality and leaves you in complete control over your codebase").
  Say what he did and what happened, and let the reader judge
- Absolutes about the right way (eg "The only viable path is..."). Prefer "the
  best path I've found", unless it is clearly a joke ("the first rule of
  writing documentation")
- Category titles. He is a generalist: no "Backend engineer", no "Staff". If a
  title is needed, "software engineer" or "engineer and architect"

### Towards preachy

- Stacks of "we need" (eg "What we need is...", "We also need...", "It needs to
  be..."). One is fine. Several in a row turns a post into a lecture. Prefer
  "what I wanted was" or "what's worked for me"
- Judging other teams (eg "a level of discipline and commitment that most
  organizations do not have appetite for"). Describe the trade-off from the
  inside, ideally with a time it happened to him
- Telling the reader what to do (eg "your agent instructions should..."). Show
  what he does and why, and let the reader borrow it

### Away from curious and optimistic

- Problem-heavy openings where the hopeful part arrives late. He writes about
  what slows teams down, so pair each problem with what he tried or what has
  become possible
- Conclusions with no open ends. Say what he is still figuring out, or what
  surprised him
- Older posts asked genuine questions; newer ones mostly state answers. A real
  question mid-post (not as a section opener) shows curiosity

## Words and framing

- "The work surrounding the code" is his phrase for the non-technical side. Don't
  enumerate it (planning, strategy, etc). The list is too big to be useful
- AI gets a light mention at most. Avoid "AI agents" in bios and site copy;
  "building products with AI" is fine
- Less Go-first framing. He still uses Go but doesn't lead with it
- Casual and simple beats polished and complete, especially in bios. Polished
  topic lists read as AI-written
- Rejected as gimmicky (don't bring these shapes back): "how teams agree on a
  direction", "Technical leadership means scaling teams and products by
  investing in teams, architecture, processes, people and communication"
